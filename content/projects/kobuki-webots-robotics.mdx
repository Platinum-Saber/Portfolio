## The competition

**IESL RoboGames 2024** is run by the Institution of Engineers Sri Lanka with the Department
of Computer Science & Engineering at the University of Moratuwa. We entered the
**University category** as **Sentinels**, a team of three: Himeth Walgampaya, Pulasthi
Udugamasooriya and me. It had three rounds: two in simulation, then a
final on real robots. Of the 29 university teams that got through the first round, 12 made
the final, and **we were one of them**.

## Round 1 - Completion (Webots)

An **e-puck** in a 2.5 m maze we built to the organisers' spec. The robot had to visit
coloured walls in a fixed order (red, yellow, pink, brown, green) **from any starting
position**, then stop. Any team that completed the task went through.

## Round 2 - Elimination (Webots)

A harder brief: **no pre-built robots**, so we built our own inside a 0.25 m box. The maze
was 5 m square, and the faculty was on fire. Three fire pits took marks off on entry and
every two seconds after that. Three survivors had to be rescued by staying in their cell for
three seconds, and then the robot had to get back out. A dry run was allowed for mapping,
the robot could not look over walls, and ties were decided on time. This round cut the field
down to the **12 finalists**.

## The final - Kobuki, Kinect, Raspberry Pi 5

This time it was a real robot: a **Kobuki** base with a **Kinect** for vision and a
**Raspberry Pi 5** running everything. The task was about colour. The robot had to find
coloured cubes, trap each one between flaps on its front, find the floor tile of the same
colour and push the cube onto it.

**Perception.** OpenCV on the Kinect's colour stream. Each colour has an HSV threshold (red
needs two, because its hue wraps around zero), with contour filtering to remove noise. One
cheap geometric rule tells cubes from tiles: a roughly square blob is a cube, and a wide,
flat blob low in the frame is a tile. A separate calibration script re-tunes the thresholds
under the venue's lighting, which never matches the lab's.

**Control.** A five-state machine: search box, approach, search tile, push, retreat. The
robot turns in small steps to centre the target and creeps forward until the target's width
in pixels says it is inside the flaps. If a colour stays hidden for 20 s, the robot skips it
and comes back to it later. Pushes are capped, so a missed tile cannot turn into pushing
forever.

**Talking to the base.** No ROS. The Pi writes raw **Kobuki serial packets** (header, then a
base-control payload) straight down the USB link. That leaves less to install on the Pi and
less to break on competition day.

## What I took from it

Moving from simulation to hardware is the real lesson. In Webots, colours are exact, wheels
don't slip and the camera has no lag. On the Kobuki, each of those becomes a problem, and
you have to choose which ones to model and which ones to just handle robustly. A lot of the
final came down to the unglamorous parts: colour calibration on site, small motion steps you
can trust, and timeouts so the robot always makes progress.
